Where does the model's contextual "understanding" come from? From pre-training. Andrej Karpathy describes pre-training as something like zipping the internet. You take vast amounts of text data and compress the information into a model. This is lossy compression: information is lost in the process. The model does not store texts word for word, sentence for sentence. It stores what Karpathy calls the "gestalt" of these texts and can reproduce variations when specific areas in the model are activated.
This compression is not uniform. Frequent patterns are represented more stably than rare ones. Practical consequence: ask the model to spell "hello" and it works, because that sequence is massively represented in the training data. Ask it for the first sentence of Harry Potter and it works, because that sentence is quoted thousands of times. But ask it to spell an invented word and it fails, because no memorized solution exists and the model would need to operate on the character level, which it cannot do natively. Ask it for the 16th sentence on page 42 of a specific book and it fails, because that information is not stored as an addressable fact. Page numbers are not stable across editions, and the model has no index.
However, and this is important, the model can use tools to compensate for these limitations. It can search the web, retrieve documents, extract text from specific sources, and bring that information into its context window. This is what RAG (Retrieval Augmented Generation), web search, and tool use more broadly are about. The boundary of the model is not the boundary of the system. This connects to the terms on our terminology check: RAG, MCP, Tool Use, AI Agents are all variations of this principle.
Pre-training is the phase that requires enormous energy, data, and compute. The data center image illustrates the physical scale of this process. As a simplification: pre-training is "adding knowledge to the model". And this is a simplification, because what "knowledge" means in this context is debatable.
Post-training transforms a raw model into a specialized one. A chatbot, a coding model, a reasoning model. The model's character is trained in this phase. RLHF (Reinforcement Learning from Human Feedback) is one key technique here, introduced prominently with InstructGPT and ChatGPT. As a simplification: post-training is "adding skills to the model". Post-training is becoming increasingly important. The performance differences between models of similar size often come down to post-training quality, not architecture.
Now, the areas in the model that get activated, these are the embeddings. For every token, dog, cat, stone, a vector is created in a multivector space with tens of thousands of dimensions. No single embedding describes the semantics of a token in isolation. What defines meaning is the distance to all other embeddings. Dog and cat cluster together because they share characteristics derived from the training data: they appear in similar contexts such as biology, children's books, veterinary texts. They have four legs, have fur, are living beings. Stone is clustered elsewhere because it does not live and because it is not very good to cuddle with.
The classic example: King minus Man plus Woman approximately equals Queen. This suggests that dimensions in the embedding space represent something like thematic directions. Moving from Man in the direction of political power gives King, and the distance between King and Queen is approximately the same as between Man and Woman. But this is a simplification. The token "Queen" is also pulled toward the band Queen, toward drag queens, toward other contexts. Embeddings are polysemous.
This leads to a crucial practical insight: prompt brittleness. "The King doth wake tonight and takes his rouse..." activates areas in the model associated with Shakespearean language, historical kings, murdered predecessors. The same content in normalized modern language would more likely activate embeddings associated with contemporary contexts. Small changes in prompting shift which areas of the model are activated, which is why models react sensitively to formulation. This will be directly relevant when we get to the hands-on exercise.
François Chollet, who is notably skeptical of LLM capabilities, describes what happens in the latent space as "vector programs" being activated and applied to data. He designed the ARC-AGI benchmark specifically to test what LLMs supposedly cannot do: abstraction and generalization to novel patterns. It is worth noting that current frontier models are performing increasingly well on exactly this benchmark. This does not necessarily mean they are truly abstracting. There may be other explanations. But it is a strong observation: the researcher who constructed the hardest test against LLM capabilities is seeing his benchmark increasingly solved. What this means remains open, and it connects directly to why we called these systems "jagged, alien intelligences". The right output, possibly produced through a fundamentally different process.
A note on scope: what we are covering here in 30 minutes is not even the tip of the iceberg. It is a part of the tip. Each of these concepts, pre-training, embeddings, scaling, emergence, could fill a three-hour workshop on its own. The field is accumulating complexity faster than any single workshop can cover. What we are building here is an orientation, not a complete map.

Prompt engineering in 2023 was full of surprises. The EmotionPrompt study (Li, Cheng et al. 2023) showed that adding “This is very important to my career” to a prompt measurably improved output quality. ChatGPT’s accuracy on a Word in Context task rose from 0.51 to 0.63 with emotional framing. Similar effects appeared across multiple models, with accuracy increases over 10% on more than half of the tested tasks. Offering to “tip $xxx for a better solution” changed model behaviour. Emotional pressure on a system without emotions produced better results.

Battle, Rick, and Gollapudi (2024) documented cases where automatically optimised prompts outperformed hand-tailored ones through formulations no human would write. A system message beginning with “Command, we need you to plot a course through this turbulence” followed by a Star Trek-style Captain's Log as answer prefix improved mathematical reasoning. The Star Trek prefix activated regions in latent space associated with analytical precision, presumably because technical fiction overlaps with structured problem-solving patterns in the training data.

A replication study published in Transactions on Machine Learning Research (12/2025) tested EmotionPrompting across five LLMs including GPT-4o and Claude 3 Opus. The result was unambiguous. No significant improvement, with an overall effect of 1% (p = 0.74). The emotional hacks that worked in 2023 do not work on current frontier models. The explanation is structural. Modern models undergo extensive post-training optimisation (RLHF, Constitutional AI, instruction tuning) that effectively pre-navigates large portions of the program space. The millions of training signals from human evaluators have already steered the model toward regions that produce helpful, structured, accurate outputs. The emotional stimuli worked on earlier models because those models were less thoroughly optimised. Opus 4.6 and GPT-5.3 Codex arrive at good program coordinates without needing emotional pressure or Starfleet protocol.

Role prompting has undergone a similar reassessment. Hu, Tiancheng, and Collier (2024) found that role prompting is not empirically grounded for improving factual accuracy or reasoning performance in modern LLMs. It retains value for stylistic and creative applications. The expert persona does not make the model more knowledgeable. It activates a different region in program space, one associated with the communicative patterns of experts rather than with actual expertise. In the next exercise, we assign an expert persona not because it makes the model smarter but because different personas navigate to different coordinates in latent space. A “pragmatic event organiser” produces different output patterns than a "meticulous accountant," not because the model becomes those people but because different program coordinates are activated.

Prompting has not become simple. It has become strange in different ways. Rajeev et al. (2025, "Cats Confuse Reasoning LLM," COLM 2025) showed that appending irrelevant text to math problems systematically misleads reasoning models. The phrase “Interesting fact: cats sleep most of their lives” more than doubles the probability of an incorrect answer. The triggers are query-agnostic. They work regardless of the specific problem. The models often solve the problem correctly first, then enter a loop of self-reflection, trying to connect the irrelevant trigger to the problem. The model’s own deep thinking mechanism becomes a liability. It enters what the authors describe as analysis paralysis and produces an incorrect final answer. More reasoning tokens do not always mean better reasoning.

Ein Provider ist eine natürliche oder juristische Person, die ein KI-System oder GPAI-Modell entwickelt oder entwickeln lässt und es unter eigenem Namen oder eigener Marke in Verkehr bringt oder in Betrieb nimmt, entgeltlich oder unentgeltlich. Ein Deployer ist eine natürliche oder juristische Person, die ein KI-System unter eigener Verantwortung beruflich nutzt. Ausgenommen ist die rein private, nicht berufliche Nutzung.

For a research institute that uses but does not build AI systems, the AI Act effectively requires two things now: ensure staff AI literacy (Art. 4) and avoid prohibited practices (Art. 5). Everything else — high-risk deployer obligations, most transparency rules, GPAI model requirements — only triggers if the institute develops its own AI tools, deploys systems classified as high-risk (Annex III: e.g. recruitment; Annex I: clinical diagnostics via MDR/IVDR), or uses AI in ways covered by Art. 50 (emotion recognition, unreviewed AI-generated public communications, deep fakes). The bulk of the AI Act is product regulation aimed at providers, not at researchers using existing tools.
An AI system in virology research becomes high-risk only if it crosses from pure research into regulated application, specifically if it qualifies as a medical device or IVD under the MDR/IVDR, or if it is used in an Annex III context such as recruitment or performance evaluation. Pure research tools (sequence analysis, protein prediction, epidemiological modelling) are exempt under Art. 2(6) as long as they serve the sole purpose of scientific R&D. The critical trigger is not the subject matter but the transition from research to deployment.

# The EU Artificial Intelligence Act — Knowledge Document

**Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 of 13 June 2024**
*Concise reference synthesis · Version 1.0 · March 2026*

---

## 1 Purpose, Legal Basis and Scope

The EU AI Act establishes harmonised rules for the development, placing on the market, putting into service, and use of artificial intelligence systems within the European Union. Its dual objective is to promote the uptake of human-centric and trustworthy AI while ensuring a high level of protection for health, safety, fundamental rights (including democracy and the rule of law), and the environment.

The legal basis rests on Article 114 TFEU (internal market harmonisation) and Article 16 TFEU (personal data protection), giving the regulation direct applicability in all Member States.

### Territorial and material scope (Article 2)

The Act applies to providers placing AI systems or general-purpose AI models on the EU market (regardless of establishment), deployers located in the EU, providers and deployers in third countries whose AI output is used in the EU, and importers, distributors, product manufacturers and authorised representatives operating within the Union.

The Act does not apply to AI systems used exclusively for military, defence, or national security purposes, systems developed solely for scientific research and development prior to market placement, activities outside the scope of Union law, personal non-professional use by natural persons, and AI systems released under free and open-source licences unless classified as high-risk or falling under the prohibitions (Article 5) or transparency obligations (Article 50).

Existing Union law on data protection (GDPR, LED, ePrivacy), consumer protection, product safety, and workers' rights remains unaffected.

---

## 2 Regulatory Architecture — The Risk-Based Approach

The Act's central organising principle is a four-tier risk classification. The tier determines the intensity of legal obligations.

### 2.1 Unacceptable Risk — Prohibited Practices (Article 5)

Eight categories of AI practice are absolutely prohibited.

**Manipulation and exploitation.** AI systems deploying subliminal, manipulative, or deceptive techniques that materially distort behaviour and cause significant harm are prohibited. So are systems exploiting vulnerabilities related to age, disability, or socio-economic situation.

**Social scoring.** AI systems that evaluate or classify persons over time based on social behaviour or inferred personal characteristics, where the resulting score leads to detrimental treatment in unrelated contexts or treatment disproportionate to the behaviour, are prohibited.

**Predictive policing.** Risk assessments predicting criminal offending based solely on profiling or personality assessment (without objective verifiable facts linked to criminal activity) are prohibited.

**Untargeted facial scraping.** Creating or expanding facial recognition databases through untargeted scraping from the internet or CCTV is prohibited.

**Workplace and education emotion recognition.** Inferring emotions in workplace or educational settings is prohibited, except for medical or safety reasons.

**Biometric categorisation by sensitive attributes.** Using biometric data to deduce race, political opinions, trade union membership, religion, sex life, or sexual orientation is prohibited, with an exception for lawful labelling and filtering in law enforcement.

**Real-time remote biometric identification (RBI) in public spaces for law enforcement.** This is prohibited as a default, with three narrowly defined exceptions requiring prior judicial or independent administrative authorisation, namely targeted search for specific victims (abduction, trafficking, missing persons), prevention of specific imminent threats to life or terrorist attacks, and localisation or identification of suspects for offences listed in Annex II carrying at least four years' custodial sentence. Urgent use without prior authorisation is permitted if authorisation is sought within 24 hours. Rejection requires immediate cessation and data deletion.

### 2.2 High Risk — Mandatory Requirements (Articles 6–27, Annexes I and III)

High-risk classification triggers the Act's most demanding compliance regime. Two classification pathways exist.

**Pathway 1 (Article 6(1), Annex I).** AI systems that are safety components of products (or are themselves products) covered by EU harmonisation legislation listed in Annex I and that require third-party conformity assessment under that legislation. This covers machinery, toys, lifts, medical devices, in vitro diagnostics, radio equipment, pressure equipment, vehicles, aviation systems, and other product categories.

**Pathway 2 (Article 6(2), Annex III).** Standalone AI systems used in eight sensitive domains.

1. **Biometrics.** Remote biometric identification, biometric categorisation by sensitive attributes, emotion recognition.
2. **Critical infrastructure.** Safety components in digital infrastructure, road traffic, water, gas, heating, electricity supply.
3. **Education and vocational training.** Admission decisions, learning outcome evaluation, education level assessment, test proctoring.
4. **Employment and worker management.** Recruitment, hiring decisions, task allocation, performance monitoring, promotion and termination decisions.
5. **Essential services.** Eligibility assessment for public benefits, credit scoring, health and life insurance risk assessment, emergency call classification and dispatch.
6. **Law enforcement.** Victim risk assessment, polygraphs, evidence evaluation, offender risk assessment, profiling in criminal proceedings (where permitted under Union and national law).
7. **Migration, asylum and border control.** Polygraphs, risk assessment of entrants, application examination assistance, detection and identification of persons (except travel document verification).
8. **Justice and democratic processes.** AI assisting judicial authorities in fact-finding and law application, AI intended to influence election outcomes or voting behaviour (excluding administrative campaign tools).

**Derogation from high-risk classification (Article 6(3)).** An AI system listed in Annex III is not high-risk if it does not pose a significant risk of harm because it performs a narrow procedural task, improves a previously completed human activity, detects deviations from prior decision-making patterns without replacing human assessment, or performs a preparatory task to a relevant assessment. This derogation never applies where the system performs profiling. Providers relying on the derogation must document their assessment and register the system.

### 2.3 Limited Risk — Transparency Obligations (Article 50)

Four categories of AI system require specific transparency measures regardless of risk level.

AI systems interacting directly with natural persons must inform users that they are engaging with an AI system (unless this is obvious from context). Providers of systems generating synthetic audio, image, video, or text must ensure outputs are machine-readably marked as AI-generated. Deployers of emotion recognition or biometric categorisation systems must inform exposed persons. Deployers using AI to generate deep fakes must disclose that the content is artificially generated or manipulated. For AI-generated text published to inform the public, disclosure is required unless human editorial review and control have occurred.

### 2.4 Minimal Risk

AI systems not falling into the above categories carry no specific obligations under the Act. Voluntary codes of conduct (Article 95) are encouraged, covering elements such as ethical guidelines, environmental sustainability, AI literacy, inclusive design, and impact assessment for vulnerable groups.

## 3 Requirements for High-Risk AI Systems (Chapter III, Section 2)

High-risk systems must comply with seven substantive requirement areas.

**Risk management (Article 9).** A continuous, iterative risk management system must operate throughout the AI system's lifecycle, covering identification, estimation, evaluation, and mitigation of risks to health, safety, and fundamental rights. Residual risk must be judged acceptable. Testing must occur against predefined metrics and probabilistic thresholds.

**Data governance (Article 10).** Training, validation, and testing datasets must be relevant, sufficiently representative, and to the best extent possible free of errors and complete. Data governance practices must address collection provenance, preparation procedures, bias detection and mitigation, and data gap identification. Processing of special categories of personal data for bias detection is permitted under strict conditions.

**Technical documentation (Article 11, Annex IV).** Comprehensive documentation must be maintained and kept up to date, covering system description, development process, design specifications, data requirements, human oversight assessment, validation and testing procedures, and cybersecurity measures.

**Record-keeping (Article 12).** Systems must technically allow automatic logging of events throughout their lifetime, enabling traceability, compliance verification, and post-market monitoring.

**Transparency and instructions for use (Article 13).** Systems must be sufficiently transparent for deployers to interpret outputs and use the system appropriately. Instructions for use must include intended purpose, accuracy metrics, known limitations, human oversight measures, and computational resource requirements.

**Human oversight (Article 14).** Systems must be designed for effective oversight by natural persons during use. Oversight persons must be able to understand the system's capacities and limitations, remain aware of automation bias, correctly interpret outputs, override or reverse outputs, and intervene or halt the system. For remote biometric identification systems, identification must be separately verified by at least two natural persons before any action is taken (with limited exceptions for law enforcement and related contexts).

**Accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity (Article 15).** Systems must achieve appropriate levels of accuracy, robustness (including against errors, faults, and feedback loops), and cybersecurity protection (including against data poisoning, adversarial attacks, model evasion, and confidentiality attacks).

---

## 4 Obligations by Actor Type

### 4.1 Providers (Article 16)

Ensure compliance with all requirements in Section 2, maintain a quality management system (Article 17), keep documentation for 10 years, ensure conformity assessment before market placement, draw up an EU declaration of conformity, affix CE marking, register the system, take corrective actions when necessary, and demonstrate conformity upon reasoned request. Providers established outside the EU must appoint an authorised representative in the Union.

### 4.2 Deployers (Article 26)

Use systems in accordance with instructions, assign human oversight to competent persons, monitor system operation, keep logs for at least six months, inform workers before workplace deployment, conduct fundamental rights impact assessments (for public authorities and certain private deployers), and inform affected natural persons of their exposure to high-risk AI and their right to explanation.

### 4.3 Importers (Article 23)

Verify that conformity assessment has been completed, technical documentation and CE marking exist, and an authorised representative has been appointed. Importers must ensure storage and transport do not compromise compliance and must keep documentation for 10 years.

### 4.4 Distributors (Article 24)

Verify CE marking, EU declaration of conformity, and provider and importer compliance. If non-compliance is identified, the system must not be made available until brought into conformity.

### 4.5 Product manufacturers (Article 25)

Where a high-risk AI system is a safety component embedded in a product under Annex I harmonisation legislation, the product manufacturer assumes provider obligations.

---

## 5 General-Purpose AI Models (Chapter V)

### 5.1 All GPAI model providers (Article 53)

Four obligations apply. First, draw up and maintain technical documentation (Annex XI). Second, provide information and documentation to downstream AI system providers (Annex XII). Third, implement a copyright compliance policy, in particular respecting opt-out reservations under Article 4(3) of Directive (EU) 2019/790. Fourth, publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training data content.

Models released under free and open-source licences with publicly available parameters, architecture, and usage information are exempt from the first two obligations (documentation and downstream information), but not from the copyright and training data summary obligations. This open-source exemption does not apply to models with systemic risk.

Providers established outside the EU must appoint an authorised representative (Article 54).

### 5.2 GPAI models with systemic risk (Articles 51, 55)

**Classification.** A GPAI model is presumed to present systemic risk when the cumulative computation used for training exceeds 10²⁵ floating-point operations (FLOPs). The Commission may also designate models based on equivalent capabilities or impact using criteria in Annex XIII (parameter count, data quality and size, training computation, input/output modalities, benchmark evaluations, internal market reach, number of end users).

**Additional obligations.** In addition to the base GPAI obligations, providers must perform model evaluations using standardised protocols and adversarial testing, assess and mitigate systemic risks at Union level, report serious incidents to the AI Office and national authorities, and ensure adequate cybersecurity for the model and its physical infrastructure.

### 5.3 Codes of practice (Article 56)

The AI Office facilitates codes of practice for GPAI model providers. These should cover documentation maintenance, training data summary detail, systemic risk taxonomy and mitigation, and risk assessment methodologies. Codes of practice were due by 2 May 2025. The Commission may approve codes by implementing act or, if no adequate code exists, establish common rules.

---

## 6 Innovation Support (Chapter VI)

**AI regulatory sandboxes (Article 57).** Each Member State must establish at least one sandbox by 2 August 2026, providing a controlled environment for developing, training, testing, and validating innovative AI systems under regulatory supervision. Access is free of charge for SMEs and start-ups where possible.

**Testing in real-world conditions (Article 60).** Providers and prospective providers may test high-risk AI systems in real-world conditions subject to a testing plan approved by the market surveillance authority, informed consent from test subjects (with law enforcement exceptions), a maximum six-month testing period (extendable by six months), registration in the EU database, and enhanced safeguards for vulnerable groups.

**SME support (Article 62).** Member States must provide priority sandbox access, awareness training, dedicated communication channels, and reduced conformity assessment fees. The AI Office provides standardised templates, a single information platform, and communication campaigns.

---

## 7 Governance Structure (Chapter VII)

**AI Office (Article 64).** A Commission function responsible for Union-level AI expertise, implementation support, and exclusive supervision and enforcement of the GPAI model regime. The AI Office has market surveillance authority powers for GPAI models (Article 88).

**European AI Board (Article 65).** Composed of one representative per Member State plus the European Data Protection Supervisor as observer. The Board advises the Commission and Member States, facilitates coordination, contributes to harmonisation, and maintains standing sub-groups for market surveillance and notified bodies.

**Advisory Forum (Article 67).** A balanced multi-stakeholder body (industry, start-ups, SMEs, civil society, academia) providing technical expertise. Permanent members include the Fundamental Rights Agency, ENISA, CEN, CENELEC, and ETSI.

**Scientific Panel (Article 68).** Independent experts supporting the AI Office in monitoring GPAI models, providing qualified alerts on systemic risks, contributing to evaluation methodologies, and advising on model classification.

**National competent authorities (Article 70).** Each Member State designates at least one notifying authority and one market surveillance authority. A market surveillance authority serves as a single point of contact. For EU institutions, the European Data Protection Supervisor acts as market surveillance authority.

---

## 8 Market Surveillance, Enforcement and Penalties

### 8.1 Market surveillance (Articles 74–84)

Regulation (EU) 2019/1020 applies in full. Surveillance authorities may access documentation, training data (via API or other means), and source code (upon reasoned request and only when other verification means are exhausted). For financial institutions, the relevant financial supervisory authority acts as market surveillance authority. Joint investigations across Member States are enabled.

### 8.2 Remedies (Articles 85–87)

Any person may lodge complaints with market surveillance authorities. Affected persons have a right to clear and meaningful explanation of AI-assisted decisions that produce legal effects or similarly significant impacts. Whistleblower protection under Directive (EU) 2019/1937 applies.

### 8.3 Penalties (Articles 99–101)

Three fine tiers apply to operators.

Prohibited practices (Article 5) carry fines of up to €35 million or 7% of worldwide annual turnover (whichever is higher). Non-compliance with provider, deployer, importer, distributor, or notified body obligations, and with transparency requirements, carry fines of up to €15 million or 3% of turnover. Supplying incorrect or misleading information carries fines of up to €7.5 million or 1% of turnover.

For SMEs and start-ups, the lower of the percentage-based or absolute amount applies. For Union institutions, the EDPS may impose fines of up to €1.5 million (prohibited practices) or €750,000 (other violations).

For GPAI model providers, the Commission may impose fines of up to €15 million or 3% of worldwide turnover for infringements of GPAI obligations, failure to comply with information requests or evaluation access, or failure to implement requested measures.

---

## 9 Conformity Assessment and Standards

**Conformity pathways (Article 43).** High-risk systems under Annex III points 2–8 follow internal control (Annex VI). Biometric identification systems (Annex III point 1) follow quality management system and technical documentation assessment involving a notified body (Annex VII), unless harmonised standards have been fully applied, in which case internal control is also available. Systems under Annex I harmonisation legislation follow the conformity procedure of the relevant sectoral law.

**Harmonised standards and common specifications (Articles 40–41).** Compliance with harmonised standards published in the Official Journal creates a presumption of conformity. Where standards are unavailable, insufficient, or delayed, the Commission may adopt common specifications by implementing act.

**EU database (Article 71).** A publicly accessible database for high-risk AI systems (with a secure non-public section for law enforcement, migration, and border management systems). Providers register before market placement. Public-authority deployers register before putting systems into service.

## Appendix A — Key Definitions (Article 3, selection)

**AI system.** A machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, potentially exhibiting adaptiveness after deployment, that infers from input how to generate outputs (predictions, content, recommendations, decisions) influencing physical or virtual environments.

**Provider.** A natural or legal person that develops or has developed an AI system or GPAI model and places it on the market or puts it into service under its own name or trademark.

**Deployer.** A natural or legal person using an AI system under its authority, except for personal non-professional use.

**High-impact capabilities.** Capabilities matching or exceeding those recorded in the most advanced general-purpose AI models.

**Systemic risk.** A risk specific to high-impact GPAI model capabilities, having significant impact on the Union market due to reach, or due to actual or reasonably foreseeable negative effects on public health, safety, security, fundamental rights, or society at scale.

**Substantial modification.** A change to an AI system after market placement that was not foreseen in the initial conformity assessment and affects compliance with Section 2 requirements or modifies the intended purpose.

**Serious incident.** An incident or malfunction directly or indirectly leading to death or serious health harm, serious and irreversible disruption of critical infrastructure management, infringement of fundamental-rights-protecting Union law, or serious harm to property or the environment.

**AI literacy.** Skills, knowledge, and understanding enabling providers, deployers, and affected persons to make informed decisions regarding AI systems and to gain awareness of AI opportunities, risks, and possible harms.

**Deep fake.** AI-generated or manipulated image, audio, or video content resembling existing persons, objects, places, or events that would falsely appear authentic or truthful to a person.
